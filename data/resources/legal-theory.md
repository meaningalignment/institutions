# Legal theory

Law as a resource for AI design: legal reasoning, agency and liability, and the infrastructure needed to govern agents.

## Selected papers

- [The Agentic Web Requires New Normative Infrastructure](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10711)
  - Authors: Cameron Pattison, Matthew Boulos, Noam Kolt, Changbai Li, Tiziano Piccardi, Seth Lazar
  - Year: 2026
  - Type: Preprint
  - Cells: dyadic-rights, community-protocols, national-rights
  - Summary: Makes the case for laws, norms, and practices that distinguish appropriately delegated agents from malicious automation on the web.

- [What Is Law? A Coordination Model of the Characteristics of Legal Order](https://doi.org/10.1093/jla/las008)
  - Authors: Gillian K. Hadfield, Barry R. Weingast
  - Year: 2012
  - Venue: Journal of Legal Analysis
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-rights, global-rights, community-norms
  - Summary: Models legal order as a coordination device: a public, stable, impartial set of rules lets decentralized actors agree on what counts as a violation and coordinate to punish it.

## Work in the field

- [A Busier Government, Not a Better One](https://paxmachina.ai/a-busier-government-not-a-better-one)
  - Authors: James Broughel
  - Year: 2026
  - Venue: Pax Machina
  - Type: Essay
  - Cells: national-expertise, national-incentives
  - Summary: Argues that AI can raise agencies' capacity without improving their decisions; unless agencies are made accountable for outcomes, more capacity yields better-looking justifications, not better governance.

- [Legal Alignment for Safe and Ethical AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04175)
  - Authors: Noam Kolt, Nicholas Caputo, Jack Boeglin, Cullen O'Keefe, Rishi Bommasani, Stephen Casper, Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar, Noah Feldman, Iason Gabriel, Gillian K. Hadfield, et al.
  - Year: 2026
  - Venue: TMLR
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-rights, national-protocols, global-rights
  - Summary: Sets out legal alignment as a field, proposing that AI systems follow lawful rules, borrow legal interpretation methods, and use legal concepts as structural scaffolding.

- [Legal Infrastructure for Transformative AI Governance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01474)
  - Authors: Gillian Hadfield
  - Year: 2026
  - Venue: PNAS
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-protocols, national-rights, global-protocols
  - Summary: Shifts attention from choosing rules to building legal systems that can generate and implement rules for frontier models and autonomous agents.

- [Alignment as Jurisprudence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08416)
  - Authors: Nicholas Caputo
  - Year: 2025
  - Venue: Yale Journal of Law and Technology
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-rights, national-norms, global-rights
  - Summary: Argues jurisprudential debates about how judges decide cases illuminate alignment, reading Constitutional AI and case-based reasoning through interpretive and analogical legal theory.

- [European Union regulations on algorithmic decision-making and a "right to explanation"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.08813)
  - Authors: Bryce Goodman, Seth Flaxman
  - Year: 2016
  - Venue: AI Magazine
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-rights, dyadic-rights, national-expertise
  - Summary: Reads the GDPR's restrictions on automated individual decision-making as creating a right to explanation, and traces what that demand asks of algorithm designers.

- [Microfoundations of the Rule of Law](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-polisci-100711-135226)
  - Authors: Gillian K. Hadfield, Barry R. Weingast
  - Year: 2014
  - Venue: Annual Review of Political Science
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-rights, global-rights, community-norms
  - Summary: Explains the rule of law as an equilibrium sustained by ordinary people coordinating to punish violations of shared, public rules, rather than by state enforcement alone.

- [The Politics of Precedent in International Law: A Social Network Application](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055414000276)
  - Authors: Krzysztof J. Pelc
  - Year: 2014
  - Venue: American Political Science Review
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: global-protocols, global-rights, global-norms
  - Summary: Shows that states litigate to shape legal precedent even where precedent is formally non-binding, treating case law as a contested institutional resource.
