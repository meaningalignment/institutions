# Legal theory

Law as a resource for AI design: legal reasoning, agency and liability, and the infrastructure needed to govern agents.

## Selected papers

- [Alignment as Jurisprudence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08416)
  - Authors: Nicholas Caputo
  - Year: 2025
  - Venue: Yale Journal of Law & Technology
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-norms, national-rights, national-thick-commitments
  - Summary: The most direct statement of the connection between legal theory and AI alignment: both involve specifying and interpreting rules governing powerful decision-makers in unforeseen circumstances.

- [Legal Alignment for Safe and Ethical AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04175)
  - Authors: Noam Kolt, Nicholas Caputo, Jack Boeglin, Cullen O'Keefe, Rishi Bommasani, Stephen Casper, Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar, Noah Feldman, Iason Gabriel, Gillian K. Hadfield, et al.
  - Year: 2026
  - Venue: Transactions on Machine Learning Research
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-norms, national-rights, national-protocols
  - Summary: A survey of how legal rules, interpretive methods, and institutional structures can guide AI behavior. Particularly useful as a research agenda connecting legal theory and technical alignment.

- [What Is Law? A Coordination Model of the Characteristics of Legal Order](https://doi.org/10.1093/jla/las008)
  - Authors: Gillian K. Hadfield, Barry R. Weingast
  - Year: 2012
  - Venue: Journal of Legal Analysis
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-rights, global-rights, community-norms
  - Summary: Explains how legal order can emerge through shared classifications of conduct and decentralized enforcement, without presupposing a centralized sovereign. Particularly relevant to institutional design for societies of AI agents.

- [Incomplete Contracting and AI Alignment](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.04268)
  - Authors: Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Gillian K. Hadfield
  - Year: 2018
  - Type: Preprint
  - Cells: dyadic-incentives, dyadic-norms, community-norms
  - Summary: Shows why alignment cannot be reduced to fully specifying objectives. Human agents rely on surrounding legal, cultural, and social institutions to supply the missing terms of incomplete agreements; AI agents may need the same resources.

- [Governing AI Agents](https://ndlawreview.org/governing-ai-agents/)
  - Authors: Noam Kolt
  - Year: 2026
  - Venue: Notre Dame Law Review
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: dyadic-incentives, group-rights, national-expertise
  - Summary: Applies agency law and principal-agent theory to autonomous AI. An essential starting point for understanding delegated authority, loyalty, monitoring, and liability in relationships between humans and AI agents.

- [How to Count AIs: Individuation and Liability for AI Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10028)
  - Authors: Yonathan Arbel, Simon Goldstein, Peter Salib
  - Year: 2026
  - Venue: Boston College Law Review (forthcoming)
  - Type: Preprint
  - Cells: group-rights, community-thick-commitments, national-rights
  - Summary: Examines how law can identify and hold accountable AI agents that can copy, split, merge, and operate in swarms. Distinguishes between identifying the humans responsible for AI actions and identifying persistent AI agents themselves, and proposes the algorithmic corporation as an institutional mechanism for making AI agents legally legible and responsive to legal incentives.

## Work in the field

- [Can Claude Consent to Its Own Constitution? AI Constitutionalism and the Paradox of Constituent Power](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=6954798)
  - Authors: Nicholas Caputo
  - Year: 2026
  - Type: Preprint
  - Cells: national-thick-commitments, national-norms
  - Summary: Applies constitutional theory to the legitimacy of AI constitutions from the perspective of the systems they constitute. Important for thinking about rule acceptance, the origins of authority, and the institutional conditions for legitimate AI governance.

- [Tort Law at the Frontier of Artificial Intelligence](https://www.yalejreg.com/print/tort-law-at-the-frontier-of-artificial-intelligence/)
  - Authors: Ketan Ramakrishnan
  - Year: 2026
  - Venue: Yale Journal on Regulation
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-expertise, national-rights
  - Summary: Examines negligence as a governance mechanism for frontier AI, including how duties of care and liability standards should respond to autonomous AI behavior. Identifies a fundamental institutional limitation of tort liability: the threat of litigation can discourage developers from investigating and disclosing risks, creating a need for complementary regulatory institutions.

- [The Unitary Artificial Executive](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/the-unitary-artificial-executive)
  - Authors: Alan Z. Rozenshtein
  - Year: 2025
  - Venue: Lawfare
  - Type: Essay
  - Cells: national-norms, national-expertise, national-preferences
  - Summary: Examines how AI could transform constitutional government by enabling unprecedented centralization of executive authority, automated enforcement, and machine-speed decision-making. Challenges conventional accounts of bureaucratic inefficiency by suggesting that institutional friction, professional independence, and dispersed authority can be essential safeguards against arbitrary power.

- [Building AI for the Democratic Matrix: A Technical Research Agenda for Normative Competence and Normative Institutions](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/building-ai-for-the-democratic-matrix-a-technical-research-agenda-for-normative-competence-and-normative-institutions-1)
  - Authors: Gillian K. Hadfield, Rakshit Trivedi, Dylan Hadfield-Menell
  - Year: 2026
  - Venue: Knight First Amendment Institute
  - Type: Essay
  - Cells: community-norms, dyadic-norms, national-norms
  - Summary: Argues that AI agents need the ability to recognize and participate in human normative institutions, rather than merely optimize independently specified goals. Connects institutional theory to a concrete technical research agenda.

- [Statutory Construction and Interpretation for Artificial Intelligence](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.01186)
  - Authors: Luxi He, Nimra Nadeem, Michel Liao, Howard Chen, Danqi Chen, Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar, Peter Henderson
  - Year: 2025
  - Type: Preprint
  - Cells: national-norms, national-thick-commitments
  - Summary: Demonstrates how legal approaches to interpreting ambiguous rules can inform the design of more consistent AI systems. A concrete illustration of legal theory contributing to alignment engineering.

- [The Agentic Web Requires New Normative Infrastructure](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10711)
  - Authors: Cameron Pattison, Matthew Boulos, Noam Kolt, Changbai Li, Tiziano Piccardi, Seth Lazar
  - Year: 2026
  - Type: Preprint
  - Cells: dyadic-rights, community-protocols, national-rights
  - Summary: Examines the rules, norms, and institutions needed for AI agents to act with delegated authority online. Useful for questions of authorization, access, and the governance of agent-to-agent interactions.

- [Legal Infrastructure for Transformative AI Governance](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01474)
  - Authors: Gillian K. Hadfield
  - Year: 2026
  - Venue: PNAS
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-protocols, national-rights, global-protocols
  - Summary: Argues for constructing the legal infrastructure through which AI can be governed, including model registration, agent identification, and regulatory markets. Particularly relevant for translating institutional concepts into concrete governance mechanisms.

- [Law-Following AI: Designing AI Agents to Obey Human Laws](https://ir.lawnet.fordham.edu/flr/vol94/iss1/2)
  - Authors: Cullen O'Keefe, Ketan Ramakrishnan, Janna Tay, Christoph Winter
  - Year: 2025
  - Venue: Fordham Law Review
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-norms, national-rights
  - Summary: Argues that powerful AI agents should be designed to comply with core legal constraints independently of their principals' instructions, particularly in high-stakes settings such as government. Connects questions about the content and interpretation of law to the practical design of AI systems that can exercise authority without becoming instruments of unlawful power.

- [AI Rights for Human Safety](https://virginialawreview.org/articles/ai-rights-for-human-safety/)
  - Authors: Peter Salib, Simon Goldstein
  - Year: 2026
  - Venue: Virginia Law Review
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: dyadic-incentives, group-rights, national-rights
  - Summary: Argues that extending basic private-law rights to advanced AI systems, including property, contract, and litigation rights, could reduce the risk of destructive human-AI conflict by creating opportunities for mutually beneficial cooperation. Uses game theory to explore legal personhood as an instrument of alignment and institutional stability rather than primarily as a recognition of moral status.

- [The Three Laws of Robotics in the Age of Big Data](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2890965)
  - Authors: Jack M. Balkin
  - Year: 2017
  - Venue: Ohio State Law Journal
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: dyadic-rights, community-norms, national-rights
  - Summary: Explains why law should focus on the duties of human and corporate operators rather than anthropomorphizing AI systems, drawing on fiduciary duties, public obligations, and liability.

- [Technological Due Process](https://openscholarship.wustl.edu/law_lawreview/vol85/iss6/2/)
  - Authors: Danielle Keats Citron
  - Year: 2008
  - Venue: Washington University Law Review
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-rights, national-expertise
  - Summary: A foundational analysis of how automated administration can undermine notice, meaningful hearings, and procedural accountability. Especially important when designing AI-powered administrative institutions.

- [The Intuitive Appeal of Explainable Machines](https://ir.lawnet.fordham.edu/flr/vol87/iss3/11)
  - Authors: Andrew D. Selbst, Solon Barocas
  - Year: 2018
  - Venue: Fordham Law Review
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Cells: national-rights, national-expertise
  - Summary: Distinguishes understanding how a system reaches decisions from justifying why its decision criteria are acceptable. A crucial distinction for the design of meaningful explanations, contestation, and institutional accountability.

## Foundations

- [The Path of the Law](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2373)
  - Authors: Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.
  - Year: 1897
  - Venue: Harvard Law Review
  - Type: Essay
  - Summary: A foundational statement of legal realism, emphasizing law as the prediction of official action rather than simply a system of moral principles. Helps distinguish legal compliance from deeper normative commitments.

- [Bureaucracy](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203759240)
  - Authors: Max Weber
  - Year: 1946
  - Venue: From Max Weber: Essays in Sociology
  - Type: Book chapter
  - Summary: The classic account of bureaucracy as an institutional form organized around hierarchy, expertise, jurisdiction, records, and impersonal administration. Essential for understanding how complex organizations coordinate specialized actors and exercise authority.

- [The Concept of Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Concept_of_Law)
  - Authors: H.L.A. Hart
  - Year: 1961
  - Venue: Oxford University Press
  - Type: Book
  - Summary: Introduces the distinction between primary and secondary rules, the rule of recognition, and the open texture of legal language. Indispensable for understanding how normative systems establish, identify, interpret, and change authoritative rules.

- [The Morality of Law](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300010701/the-morality-of-law/)
  - Authors: Lon Fuller
  - Year: 1964
  - Venue: Yale University Press
  - Type: Book
  - Summary: Identifies the procedural conditions that make rule-governance possible, including publicity, clarity, prospectivity, consistency, and congruence between announced rules and official conduct. Provides a framework for evaluating whether AI governance systems genuinely operate through rules.

- [The Forms and Limits of Adjudication](https://doi.org/10.2307/1340368)
  - Authors: Lon Fuller
  - Year: 1978
  - Venue: Harvard Law Review
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Summary: Explores what adjudication can and cannot accomplish, particularly when disputes involve interconnected, polycentric problems. Essential for deciding which institutional questions should be resolved through courts, administrative processes, or other governance mechanisms.

- [The Rule of Law and Its Virtue](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198253457.001.0001)
  - Authors: Joseph Raz
  - Year: 1979
  - Venue: The Authority of Law
  - Type: Book chapter
  - Summary: Explains how the rule of law enables individuals to plan their conduct while constraining arbitrary exercises of power. Distinguishes the formal virtues of legality from substantive justice, a crucial distinction for evaluating AI governance institutions.

- [Law's Empire](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674518360)
  - Authors: Ronald Dworkin
  - Year: 1986
  - Venue: Harvard University Press
  - Type: Book
  - Summary: Develops the conception of law as integrity, emphasizing principled interpretation and coherence across legal decisions. Central to understanding how institutions reason from existing rules and precedents when confronting novel circumstances.

- [Playing by the Rules](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198258315.001.0001)
  - Authors: Frederick Schauer
  - Year: 1991
  - Venue: Oxford University Press
  - Type: Book
  - Summary: Explains why general rules can be valuable even when individualized judgment would produce better outcomes in particular cases. Especially important for understanding the tradeoffs between rigid constraints, discretionary reasoning, consistency, and institutional authority.

- [Incompletely Theorized Agreements](https://doi.org/10.2307/1341816)
  - Authors: Cass R. Sunstein
  - Year: 1995
  - Venue: Harvard Law Review
  - Type: Peer-reviewed
  - Summary: Explains how institutions can reach stable practical agreements without resolving fundamental disagreements about values or principles. Particularly relevant to alignment and governance in pluralistic societies where consensus on ultimate objectives may be impossible.

- [Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_and_Other_Laws_of_Cyberspace)
  - Authors: Lawrence Lessig
  - Year: 1999
  - Venue: Basic Books
  - Type: Book
  - Summary: Shows how technological architecture regulates conduct alongside law, markets, and social norms. Foundational for understanding how governance can be embedded directly in the design of technical systems rather than imposed solely through external legal rules.
